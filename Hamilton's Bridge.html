Where it starts

Multiplying by a complex number is a turn

A complex number z = a + bi is a point (a, b) in the plane, with the single rule i² = −1. Adding complex numbers adds the points like arrows. Multiplying them is where the geometry is.

Write any complex number in polar form: a length r = |w| and an angle θ, so w = r(cos θ + i sin θ). Then multiplying z by w does two things at once:

  • it scales z by r, so lengths multiply: |zw| = |z| |w|;
  • it rotates z by θ, so angles add: arg(zw) = arg(z) + arg(w).

So a complex number of length 1 is a rotation of the plane. Multiplying by i is exactly a quarter turn, since i = cos 90° + i sin 90°. Doing it twice turns 180°, which is multiplying by −1. That is the geometric reason i² = −1.

Drag z around and change w. Try w = i and watch z step round in four quarter turns.

The faint dots are z, zw, zw², … With |w| = 1 they stay on one circle. Any other length makes a spiral.

The same thing as a matrix

Multiplying by w = a + bi is a linear map of the plane with matrix

[[a, −b], [b, a]] = r · [[cos θ, −sin θ], [sin θ, cos θ]]

A rotation matrix times a scale. Complex numbers are a compact way of writing exactly these matrices, and multiplying them in either order gives the same answer.

The conjugate is a mirror

The conjugate z̄ = a − bi reflects z in the real axis. Multiplying a number by its own mirror cancels the angle and leaves the squared length:

z z̄ = a² + b² = |z|²,  so  z⁻¹ = z̄ / |z|²

For a unit complex number the inverse is simply the conjugate: turning back is the same as turning by −θ. Quaternions keep exactly this rule.

The exponential

Why eiθ lands on the circle

Euler's formula says eiθ = cos θ + i sin θ. You can watch it happen. The exponential is the sum 1 + x + x²/2! + x³/3! + …, and with x = iθ each term is the previous one multiplied by iθ/n.

Multiplying by i is a quarter turn, so each new term points 90° left of the one before, and gets shorter once n exceeds θ. Added head to tail, the terms curl inward and settle on the point at angle θ on the unit circle. Add terms one at a time and watch the spiral close in.

Real terms (1, −θ²/2!, θ⁴/4!, …) are drawn in ink, imaginary ones in orange. Only one fact was used: i² = −1. Keep that in mind for later.

The problem

Hamilton wanted triples, and triples don't work

If two numbers describe turns in the plane, three should describe turns in space. For over a decade Hamilton tried to multiply triples a + bi + cj, with i² = j² = −1, so that lengths multiply the way they do for complex numbers.

The sticking point is the product ij. In a system of triples it has to be another triple, say ij = a + bi + cj. Now multiply both sides on the left by i. If multiplication is associative, the left side is (ii)j = −j. The right side expands to

i(a + bi + cj) = (ca − b) + (a + cb)i + c²j

For the two sides to match, the j part needs c² = −1. Try it yourself: pick any a, b, c and see whether the right side can reach −j.

needed for −jwhat your guess gives

No real c squares to −1, so no triple system works, at least none that keeps associativity. Hurwitz later proved the general version in 1898: a multiplication that preserves length exists only in dimensions 1, 2, 4 and 8. Three is not on the list.

Walking along the Royal Canal on 16 October 1843, Hamilton saw the way out. Stop forcing ij back into the triple. Give it its own new direction k, work in four dimensions, and give up the rule that pq = qp. He cut the rules into the stone of the bridge he was crossing.

The rules

Three square roots of −1 that don't commute

A quaternion is q = w + xi + yj + zk, four real numbers. Everything follows from the one line on the bridge, i² = j² = k² = ijk = −1. For example, multiply ijk = −1 on the right by k:

ijkk = −k ⇒ ij(−1) = −k ⇒ ij = k

The same trick gives jk = i and ki = j, and reversing any pair flips the sign. Tap any cell of the table to compare the product both ways round.

row × column

The same pattern as the cross product of unit vectors: x × y = z, y × z = x, z × x = y. That is no coincidence, as the next section shows.

Conjugate and length, exactly as in ℂ

The conjugate flips the sign of all three imaginary parts. A quaternion times its conjugate is again its squared length, a plain real number:

q* = w − xi − yj − zk,  q q* = w² + x² + y² + z² = |q|²

So every nonzero quaternion has an inverse, q⁻¹ = q* / |q|², and for a unit quaternion q⁻¹ = q*. You can divide, just as in ℂ.

What changes: order

Lengths still multiply, |pq| = |p| |q|, which is what Hamilton wanted. The price is that pq and qp are usually different. One consequence to remember:

(pq)* = q* p*,  (pq)⁻¹ = q⁻¹ p⁻¹

The order reverses, like taking off socks and shoes. Rotations in space behave the same way, which is exactly why quaternions fit them.

The relation

The complex numbers are still in there, many times over

Quaternions don't replace complex numbers. They contain them, in four different ways that each say something useful.

A copy of ℂ along every axis

Drop j and k and what remains, a + bi, multiplies exactly like ℂ. But i is not special. For any unit vector (x, y, z) the pure quaternion u = xi + yj + zk also squares to −1:

u² = −(x² + y² + z²) = −1

There is a whole sphere of square roots of −1, and each one spans its own complex plane {a + bu}. The next demo lets you pick one.

Euler's formula, with any axis

The spiral in the Euler demo used only i² = −1. Since u² = −1 too, the same series gives

euφ = cos φ + u sin φ

Unit complex numbers are eiφ, a circle. Unit quaternions are euφ for every axis u: a 3-sphere in 4D, made of great circles, one complex circle per direction.

Non-commutativity is only the cross product

Write q as a scalar and a vector, (s, v). Then

(s, v)(t, w) = (st − v·w,  sw + tv + v×w)

If v and w point the same way, v×w = 0 and the product is ordinary complex multiplication. Order only matters when the axes differ. This formula is also where the dot and cross products of vector algebra came from.

A pair of complex numbers

Since ij = k, every quaternion splits as

(a + bi) + (c + di)j = z₁ + z₂ j

Just as ℂ is a pair of reals, ℍ is a pair of complex numbers (the Cayley–Dickson construction). As a 2 × 2 complex matrix, q ↦ [[z₁, z₂], [−z̄₂, z̄₁]], with determinant |q|². Unit quaternions become the group SU(2) of quantum spin. The calculator below shows this matrix live.

Order matters, for rotations and for quaternions

Pick up a book. Turn it 90° about one axis, then 90° about another. Start again and do the two turns the other way round. The book ends up in a different pose. Rotations in 3D do not commute, so any algebra that describes them can't commute either. That is why Hamilton had to give up pq = qp.

The payoff

Turning space with q v q*

Store a vector as a pure quaternion v = xi + yj + zk. To turn it by θ about the unit axis u, build

q = cos(θ/2) + u sin(θ/2) = euθ/2,  v′ = q v q*

That is the whole recipe. Here is why it works, and why the angle is halved. Split v into a part along the axis and a part perpendicular to it, v = v∥ + v⊥.

  • The part along the axis stays put. v∥ is a multiple of u, so it lives in the same complex plane as q and commutes with it. Then q v∥q* = v∥q q* = v∥.
  • The perpendicular part turns by the full angle. Perpendicular pure quaternions anticommute: v⊥u = −uv⊥. That lets q* slide past v⊥ by becoming q: v⊥q* = q v⊥. So q v⊥q* = q²v⊥, and q² = cos θ + u sin θ.

In the plane perpendicular to the axis, the rotation is plain complex multiplication by euθ, with u playing the role of i. The half angles from the two sides add up to the full angle. Multiplying on one side only would also mix in a real part and push v out of 3D space. You can see that in the q v row of the readout.

Worked example: a quarter turn about k

Turn the vector i (the x axis) by 90° about k (the z axis). It should land on j. Half of 90° is 45°, so q = (1 + k)/√2.

build qq = cos 45° + k sin 45° = (1 + k)/√2,  q* = (1 − k)/√2 left sideq i = (i + ki)/√2 = (i + j)/√2ki = j right side(q i) q* = ½ (i + j)(1 − k) = ½ (i + j − ik − jk) simplify= ½ (i + j + j − i) = jik = −j, jk = i

Watch 360° in the lab. The dart is back where it started, yet q = −1, not 1. Only at 720° does q return home. Both q and −q give the same rotation, since the two signs cancel in q v q*. Unit quaternions cover every rotation exactly twice. Physicists call this the spinor property; electrons behave the same way, and you can feel it with the "plate trick": hold a plate flat on your palm and turn it one full turn, and your arm is twisted. A second full turn untwists it.

Gimbal lock, and why quaternions avoid it

The obvious way to describe an orientation is three angles: yaw about the vertical, then pitch about the new side axis, then roll about the nose. Each angle is a gimbal ring, and the rings are nested. The trouble starts when the middle angle, pitch, reaches ±90°. The roll axis then lies along the yaw axis, and the yaw and roll sliders turn the dart the same way. One of the three degrees of freedom is gone.

Try it. Press "Pitch to 90°", then move yaw and roll in turn. No combination of the two will tip the nose sideways. A real aircraft can still turn that way, but these three numbers can't describe it smoothly from here. The quaternion of the same pose has no such problem. It is just a point on the 3-sphere, and every direction of motion from it is available.

Blending two orientations: slerp against Euler angles

Animation, camera paths and spacecraft slews all need to get smoothly from one orientation to another. With Euler angles the natural move is to blend each angle separately. It gets there, but on a detour, speeding up and slowing down on the way.

With quaternions, both orientations are points on the unit 3-sphere. Slerp (spherical linear interpolation) walks the great circle between them at constant speed:

slerp(qA, qB, t) = qA (qA* qB)t

This is the same idea as eiθt sweeping round a circle, now in the complex plane that contains both orientations. The solid dart below follows slerp; the outlined orange one blends the Euler angles. Their nose paths are traced in the same colors.

In practice

Why spacecraft, game engines and telescopes use them

No gimbal lock

Three Euler angles lose a degree of freedom when two gimbals line up, as the demo above shows. A unit quaternion has no such singular pose; every orientation is a regular point on the 3-sphere.

Four numbers, cheap to repair

A rotation matrix stores nine numbers and drifts away from orthogonal under rounding. A quaternion stores four, and fixing drift is one division by its length.

Composition is multiplication

Rotate by q₁, then by q₂: the combined rotation is q₂q₁, 16 multiplications against 27 for 3 × 3 matrices. The order matters, exactly as it does for real rotations.

Smooth blending

Slerp gives constant-speed, shortest-path turning between two poses. Star trackers, attitude filters and animation rigs all rely on it. The dart at the top of this page is doing it, forever.

The family

Each doubling costs a property

Repeat the pair-of-numbers trick and you climb a ladder: reals, complex numbers, quaternions, octonions, sedenions. Each step doubles the dimension and gives something up. Going from ℝ to ℂ you lose the ordering (no "bigger" complex number). From ℂ to ℍ you lose commutativity. From ℍ to 𝕆 you lose associativity. After that, even division fails.

ℍ is named for Hamilton. Quaternions lost the battle for 19th-century physics to Gibbs and Heaviside's vector notation, whose dot and cross products are the two halves of the quaternion product. They came back in the second half of the 20th century for spacecraft attitude control, and in 1985 Ken Shoemake introduced slerp to computer animation.
